Selecting co-solvents for SFE using Hansen Solubility

Introduction

This vignette introduces the automated co-solvent selection and ranking workflow for Supercritical Fluid Extraction (SFE) processes, under the assumption that the reader is familiar with the technology. The choice of co-solvent composition and volume fraction (relative to CO2 flow), or indeed the choice of whether to use a co-solvent at all, is complicated by the different solubility of a target compound(s) contained in the extractable raw material, which is governed by its cohesive energy density, itself dependent on the cohesive energy and molar volume. The Hansen Solubility Theory neatly defines the cohesive energy density of any given molecule (be it solute or solvent) as a set of solubility parameters (HSPs) describing the Dispersion, Polarity, and Hydrogen Bonding. These may be derived either experimentally via solubility trials or theoretically using Group Contribution Methods based on automated detection of functional groups from SMILES strings, SMARTS notation, and other molecular descriptors.

The official website of the Hansen Solubility Theory and the accompanying book provide comprehensive information about the theory, while several real world applications (e.g. here, here, and here) give specific examples of its usefulness. In lieu of reciting their content herein, the reader is directed to familiarize themselves with both SFE and Hansen Solubility using the available sources. This vignette instead aims to provide a worked example(s) showcasing the capability of supeRcrit and the workflow encompassed by the sfe_mod function to help:

  1. Retrieve molecular descriptors of a target compound.
  2. Estimation of boiling point, critical parameters, and HSPs.
  3. Estimate performance of various co-solvent for said compound.
  4. Comparison of a single co-solvent for multiple targets, pressures, temperatures, and/or volume fractions.

Worked examples

Estimation of boiling point, critical parameters, and HSPs

The determination of co-solvent performance as target compound miscibility enhancers in supercritical CO2 requires estimation of their critical temperature and HSP values. Similarly to mol_find, the sfe_mod function handles this automatically, but a separate routine est_gcm provides this useful functionality as well. The function implements the Joback-Reid, Stein and Brown, Nannoolal, Stefanis and Panayiotou, and Hukkerikar group contribution methods (GCMs) for estimating critical parameters and Hansen Solubility. Additionally, van der waals volume (in L mole-1) can be estimated by the methods of Slonimskii, Bondi, and Zhao. Once again using beta-carotene as an example:

gcm_res <- est_gcm(solute = mol_data,
                   tb = "SB_corr", #The corrected Stein and Brown method for boiling point estimation
                   crit = "JR", #Joback-Reid method for boiling point estimation
                   hsp = "SP12", #Stefanis and Panayiotou method for HSP estimation
                   vdw = "ZHAO", #Zhao method for van der Waals volume (VDW) estimation
                   silent = TRUE)

All GCM approaches require fragmentation of a molecular structure into discrete representative groups where overlapping atoms between identified groups are either permitted or disallowed depending on the selected method. Manual group fragmentation may be time-consuming and in this package various automatic fragmentation approaches are instead employed, most of these were inspired by the work of Peter Ertl and Simon Müller. The simplicity argument (rather fittingly) determines the level of simplicity of the automatic fragmentation algorithm. Besides the default setting, "auto", the three possible values are:

  • "simple": Group overlap is allowed.
  • "normal": Group overlap is not allowed, with the first valid fragmentation pattern accepted.
  • "complex": Group overlap is not allowed and every possible fragmentation pattern is computed before final selection.

Note that the above are overridden for second- and third-order groups, where the parameters of fragmentation are the same as those originally published (for more detail, the reader is referred to the primary literature linked throughout this document). For some molecules, the "complex" approach may take hours to settle on a single fragmentation pattern out of possible thousands, but for others (like 2-methylphenanthrene exemplified below) the routine is surprisingly fast. Nevertheless, the remaining two approaches are significantly more practical, but still differ in execution time and performance:

#Define a new molecule which should contain groups of multiple orders
mol_data <- c("2531-84-2", "2-methylphenanthrene")
mol <- mol_find(mol_data)

#Compare the fragmentation time using the Hukkerikar approach for critical parameter estimation
taken <- c()
start_1 <- Sys.time()
res_simple <- est_gcm(solute = mol_data, crit = "HKR_STW", simplicity = "simple", silent = TRUE)
end_1 <- Sys.time()
taken["simple"] <- round(end_1 - start_1, 2)

#For the 'normal' simplicity level
start_2 <- Sys.time()
res_normal <- est_gcm(solute = mol_data, crit = "HKR_STW", simplicity = "normal", silent = TRUE)
end_2 <- Sys.time()
taken["normal"] <- round(end_2 - start_2, 2)

#For the 'complex' simplicity level
start_3 <- Sys.time()
res_complex <- est_gcm(solute = mol_data, crit = "HKR_STW", simplicity = "complex", silent = TRUE)
end_3 <- Sys.time()
taken["complex"] <- round(end_3 - start_3, 2)
taken
#>  simple  normal complex 
#>    8.05    6.49    7.26

We see that, somewhat surprisingly, the simple approach is slower than the more complex normal setting, while the complex fragmentation is only slightly more time-consuming for such a small molecule. Let us check the results in more detail:

reslist <- list(res_simple, res_normal, res_complex)

#Retrieve and show estimated critical temperatures

#>  simple_Tc  normal_Tc complex_Tc 
#>   859.9909   859.9909   859.9909

#Show the number of UNIQUE identified groups

We see that the simple method likely vastly overestimates the critical temperature due to the overabundance of identified groups, while there is no need for the complex approach in this case since it results in the same estimation as the more time-efficient normal approach.

res1 <- est_gcm(solute = mol_data, hsp = "SP12", gorder = 0, silent = TRUE)

#Consider only first-order groups
res2 <- est_gcm(solute = mol_data, hsp = "SP12", gorder = 1, silent = TRUE)

Estimation of co-solvent performance for one compound

The sfe_mod routine uses the Hansen Solubility Theory to estimate the relative improvement in the miscibility of a target compound (the so-called % Miscibility Enhancement) in a binary CO2 : co-solvent system of a specified composition. This allows one to determine whether addition of a specific co-solvent(s) at various concentrations improves the solubility of a target compound relative to pure supercritical CO2, and how said improvements compare between different solvents.

The general approach is based on the work of Diego Tirado and involves several steps:

  1. Calculation of the temperature influence on the HSP of the target compound using [Jayasri & Yaseen][jyas] nomograms.
  2. Calculation of mole fractions (from the provided volume fraction) of the binary system.
  3. Derivation of the average molar volume at a specified range of process temperatures and pressures from the binary system composition.
  4. Calculation of the influence of the molar volume at each set of conditions on the HSP parameters of the binary system.
  5. Calculation of the solubility parameter distance, Ra, for pure CO2 and each binary CO2 : co-solvent system.
  6. Conversion of the Ra metric into % Miscibility Enhancement relative to pure CO2.

The solvent systems are finally ranked at each possible set of conditions within the user-specified range, and the system providing the largest miscibility enhancement is chosen as the “best”.

Let us determine the miscibility enhancement for xanthohumol, a prenylflavonoid commonly found in hops and poorly soluble in supercritical CO2, using various co-solvents at a volume fraction of 0.1.

A machine-learned solubility workflow

The Hansen workflow above ranks co-solvents by a relative miscibility metric (the change in the HSP distance Ra). supeRcrit also ships a complementary, fully data-driven route that predicts the absolute supercritical-CO2 solubility of a solute — and the enhancement a co-solvent provides — directly from a SMILES string. These models are written in native R (no Python dependency) and reimplement the “thermodynamics-informed machine learning” approach of Makarov et al., trained on large open experimental datasets.

Estimating molecular properties from SMILES

Two gradient-boosted-tree estimators predict pure-component properties that are useful both downstream and in their own right. The melting point gcm_mp is trained on the open Jean-Claude Bradley dataset (~24k compounds); the enthalpy of vaporization gcm_hvap shares the same feature pipeline:

gcm_mp("CC(=O)Nc1ccc(O)cc1")          # paracetamol, ~441 K (experimental 442 K)
#> [1] 445.6656
#> attr(,"model")

gcm_mp("CC(=O)Nc1ccc(O)cc1", "C")     # the same value expressed in Celsius
#> [1] 172.5156
#> attr(,"model")
#> [1] "xgboost | Bradley(~24k full)+CHAOS sigma-profile, xgboost (n=23698, test RMSE 40 K, MAE 27 K, R2 0.81)"
gcm_hvap("CCO")                        # ethanol, ~35 kJ/mol

Predicting supercritical-CO2 solubility

predict_solubility returns the equilibrium solubility (solute mole fraction y2 in the CO2-rich phase) over a temperature/pressure grid, optionally with a liquid co-solvent at a given mole fraction. It needs only the solute SMILES and operating conditions — the melting point is estimated with gcm_mp when not supplied, or can be passed explicitly via Tm:

#Naphthalene in pure CO2 at 308 K across a pressure range
predict_solubility("c1ccc2ccccc2c1", temp = 308, pres = c(100, 150, 200, 250))
#>   temp_K pres_bar   log10_y   mole_frac
#> 1    308      100 -2.613637 0.002434235
#> 2    308      150 -2.437853 0.003648775
#> 3    308      200 -2.271966 0.005346059
#> 4    308      250 -2.193202 0.006409121

#Paracetamol with 5 mol% methanol as a co-solvent
predict_solubility("CC(=O)Nc1ccc(O)cc1", temp = 318, pres = 200, cosolvent = "CO", x = 0.05)
#>   temp_K pres_bar   log10_y    mole_frac
#> 1    318      200 -4.697251 2.007933e-05

The model is reliable within roughly 308–373 K and 80–360 bar. Mole-fraction solubilities convert to a mass loading (g of solute per kg of CO2) with solub_gkg:

solub_gkg(1.7e-4, 151.2)               # paracetamol-like, ~0.58 g/kg
#> [1] 0.5841484

Ranking co-solvents by predicted solubility enhancement

solub_optim is the solubility analogue of hsp_optim: for one solute it evaluates predict_solubility over a pressure/temperature grid for pure CO2 and for each candidate co-solvent, returning each co-solvent’s enhancement ratio (y2 with co-solvent divided by y2 in pure CO2) together with the absolute solubility. SMILES for the bundled Hansen solvents are available from show_solv():

cs <- setNames(show_solv()$SMILES, show_solv()$Solvent)

sc <- solub_optim("CC(=O)Nc1ccc(O)cc1",
                  cosolvents = cs[c("Ethanol", "Acetone", "Methanol", "Hexane")],
                  temp_C = c(40, 50, 60), pres_bar = c(150, 200, 250), x = 0.05)

#Mean enhancement of each co-solvent over the grid (higher is better)
sort(sapply(sc$enhancement, mean), decreasing = TRUE)
#>   Ethanol   Acetone    Hexane  Methanol 
#> 15.402320 14.251381  9.682389  9.466199

For paracetamol the polar alcohols and ketone (ethanol, acetone, methanol) rank well above the non-polar hexane — consistent with experiment. This also corrects a known limitation of the purely HSP-based route, which can rank a solvent that is chemically similar to the solute but barely miscible with CO2 (such as water) too highly, because Ra is blind to the co-solvent’s actual solubilising power.

Solubility curves and separator-cascade fractionation

Once each target’s solubility can be evaluated — by the density-based Chrastil correlation, the predictive COSMO-SAC-Phi equation of state, the machine-learned model above, or experimentally-fitted coefficients — supeRcrit can simulate an ideal depressurisation cascade. The extract-laden CO2 passes through a series of separators at progressively lower pressure; at each stage whatever exceeds the new (lower) saturation limit precipitates. Because different compounds have different solubility curves, they deposit preferentially in different separators, which is the basis for fractionation.

The Chrastil solubility curve over a grid is given by chrastil_solubility, and the cascade by the internal engine function supeRcrit:::chrastil_cascade (the fractionation engines are driven interactively from the Shiny Separator Cascade module). Here we take two compounds from the bundled Chrastil database (chrastil_db):

db <- chrastil_db()
sub <- head(db[is.finite(db$mw) & is.finite(db$k), ], 2)
solutes <- data.frame(name = sub$name, k = sub$k, A = sub$a, B = sub$b,
                      mw = sub$mw, basis = "mol/mol", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)

#Three separators at 40 C and decreasing pressure
stages <- data.frame(temp_C = 40, pres_bar = c(300, 180, 100))

casc <- supeRcrit:::chrastil_cascade(solutes, stages, co2_flow = 10,
                         extract = list(temp_C = 40, pres_bar = 350))

casc$deposit        # kg/h deposited per solute (rows) at each separator (columns)
#>                                     [,1]        [,2]         [,3]
#> 1-aminoanthraquinone        7.611139e-05 0.000212085 0.0002206299
#> 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene 9.128144e-02 0.254031671 0.2633876091
casc$stage_purity   # mass fraction of each separator's deposit
#>    1-aminoanthraquinone 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene
#> S1         0.0008331154                   0.9991669
#> S2         0.0008341798                   0.9991658
#> S3         0.0008369612                   0.9991630

The mass balance itself is independent of the solubility model: the companion engine supeRcrit:::solub_cascade performs the same equilibrium-stage calculation from a precomputed solute × stage solubility matrix, so a cascade can equally be driven by CSP-2021, the machine-learned model, or experimental data instead of Chrastil.

All of these workflows are also available interactively in the Shiny dashboard (run_app()): the Solute Characterization, Co-Solvent Selection/Comparison, and Solubility & Separator Cascade modules expose the GCM, HSP, melting-point/Hvap, ML-solubility, and cascade tools with figures, downloadable tables, and mol/mol ↔︎ g/kg unit toggles.
